Failure modes

Most of the ways a video file dies

Almost every camera and recorder writes the index for a clip at the very end of the take. Anything that stops the recording before that moment leaves every frame on the card and no map to find them by. That is almost every “dead” file we see.

01

Battery died mid-take

The classic. The camera goes dark, the clip never gets its index, and your editor reports the file as unreadable.

02

Card or SSD pulled

A USB-C SSD cable knocked out, a card ejected a second early. The frames are on the media; the file was never closed.

03

Camera locked up

A firmware freeze at the end of a take, a hard power-cycle, and a half-written container nothing will open.

04

Media couldn't keep up

A card too slow for the bitrate you chose. Dropped-frame warnings, and a clip where some frames were only partly written.

05

Card formatted

Format is a paperwork change, not an erase. Until a new take overwrites it, the footage is still physically there.

06

Offload interrupted

A copy that stopped halfway leaves a file of the right size that plays absolutely nothing.

07

Plays, then stops

The clip opens, runs for a few seconds and freezes or goes black. The index only covers part of what was recorded.

08

0 KB or “media offline”

The file system lost its pointer to the data. The data itself usually hasn't gone anywhere.

The process

Four steps, and you know the price before the third

This is the whole service. There is no stage where you hand over your footage and hope, and no stage where you pay for a maybe.

  1. 01

    Scan

    Open the broken file in Antidote. It reads the raw bytes, finds every frame that made it onto the card, and shows you what it found — how many seconds survived, at what resolution and codec. Nothing has left your computer at this point.

  2. 02

    Diagnose

    Send a sample of about two megabytes. An engineer — not a script — works out what the camera was doing at the moment it stopped, confirms what is recoverable, and sends back a fixed quote for that clip. Free, usually within minutes. Forty-eight hours at the very outside.

  3. 03

    Pay

    If the quote works for you, we send an invoice for exactly that amount — no subscription, no diagnosis fee, nothing added later. From $49. In the rare case the repair doesn't work out — about one job in a hundred — you get a full refund.

  4. 04

    Release

    Once it's paid, the repair — built for your camera and your specific failure — runs on your own machine, against your original file, and the repaired clip lands on your disk, ready for your editor. The engine that drives it stays on our side; your footage stays on yours.

Questions

The ones everybody asks

Can I trust a repair service I found on the internet?

You shouldn't have to take our word for it. The diagnosis is free and comes back as a plain report — how many seconds survived, at what resolution and codec — with a fixed price for that clip. If the repair then can't be done — it happens about once in a hundred jobs — you get a full refund. You never pay for a maybe, and your footage never leaves your computer while you decide.

Do I have to upload the whole clip?

No — and you never upload the clip at all. Antidote sends a sample of about two megabytes for the diagnosis, and the repair itself runs on your own computer against the original file. A long 4K take is tens of gigabytes; moving that over hotel Wi-Fi is exactly what we designed around.

How long does it take?

Most requests are answered in minutes. Our stated turnaround is up to forty-eight hours, which covers unusual camera setups and failures that need an engineer to dig.

What does it cost?

From $49 for a single clip, adjusted for resolution and duration. Whole cards and large batches are quoted at a flat rate. The diagnosis is free in every case, and the quote is fixed before you pay.

When do I pay?

After the diagnosis and before the repair. Once you have the report and the quote, we send an invoice for exactly that amount; when it's paid, the repair runs on your machine and the clip is yours. Nothing is charged before you've seen the price, and nothing is charged if the clip can't be saved.

Will I get the original file back, or a transcode?

The real thing. The repaired clip is the same container and codec at the original resolution and bit depth, with the camera metadata and audio intact — nothing re-encoded, nothing generated. It cuts and grades exactly like the take that didn't fail.

What if it can't be repaired?

Then you get your money back, in full. It happens in about one job out of a hundred — the diagnosis catches almost everything before a quote is even sent — but some failures, like a card physically overwritten by the next day's shoot, genuinely have nothing left to recover. When that's the case we refund the payment and tell you exactly why.

Which formats do you repair?

The containers cameras write — MP4, MOV, MXF, MTS, AVI, MKV and their relatives — carrying H.264, HEVC, ProRes, DNx, XAVC and most other production codecs. If yours isn't on the list, send the request anyway and say what it is: the diagnosis is free, and we'll tell you honestly whether we can help.

Is my footage confidential?

It doesn't leave your computer. The repair runs locally on your original file; the engine that drives it runs on our servers and never receives your picture. The small diagnosis sample is deleted when the job closes, and we'll sign an NDA for productions that need one.
